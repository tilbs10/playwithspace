## The Setup

There wasn't one. Our first game of the season came before our first training session — no shared language, no drills, nothing taught. Just four players, a ball, and a whistle.

Two of them had played basketball before. For the other two, this was their first real game; their background is netball.

So I made it the plan. If I wasn't going to coach anything, I could at least watch everything. What does a group of Year 4 girls do with space when nobody has told them anything yet?

## What I Saw

**They could all track the ball.** Every one of them had a good nose for it. That's a better starting point than it sounds — you can't read space if you can't follow the ball, and nobody had to be taught to look.

**They stood where the defence was.** The natural tendency, without exception: walk to wherever the defenders had set up and stand there. Nobody left space open. There was no concept of keeping a driving lane clear, because there was no concept that a lane was something you could *protect*.

**Dribbling ran the game.** At this age that's normal. It also means there's a lot sitting untouched — the gap between a team that can pass and a team that can only dribble is enormous, and passing is very coachable.

**On defence, everyone went to the ball.** It worked, sometimes — it produced a few steals. But it left a huge amount of space behind them, and the other team got clear lanes to the basket. They were defending the ball when they should have been defending the *basket* and letting the attack come to them.

## The Moment

Not a single play. The moment was the arc of the whole game.

They were nervous before it, and it showed. The physicality was new — they weren't used to the contact, and early on you could see it. But by the end they looked like a different team from the one that started. Nobody had coached them. They just played, noticed, and adjusted.

That's the best thing I could have asked for from a game with no preparation.

## What It Teaches

Every one of these defaults — bunching with the defence, chasing the ball, dribbling because it's the only thing available — is exactly what you'd expect from beginners, and none of it is a flaw in the kids. It's what happens when nobody has taught them that where you stand is a decision.

It's also a clean look at what the starting point is. [Spacing is the most undertaught fundamental](/blog/why-spacing-is-undertaught) for this reason: left alone, kids walk toward the ball and the defence, because that's where the action looks like it is. The bunched picture in [Same Game, Bigger Pitch](/blog/same-game-bigger-pitch) isn't a hypothetical — I just watched it happen.

And the improvement across the game matters most. These players can learn from what they see. That's the whole bet this season is built on.

## Next Session Focus

One thing: **leave the space open.** Before any plays, before passing technique, before defensive rotations — get them to feel what it's like to stand somewhere *useful* instead of somewhere *crowded*, and to notice what that opens up.

Passing and defending the basket come next. But the first question for session one is simple: *where is the space, and are you standing in it?*
