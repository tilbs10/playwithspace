## The Setup

This was the rematch. The same team that beat us 38-6 in round one, with five players this time.

The scoreline is worth a look before anything else: 17-25 is a loss, and it's also an eight-point game against a team that beat us by thirty-two. I'd be careful reading too much into that — the group is different, and so is the lineup — but it's real, and I'll take it.

## What I Saw

**They denied our best player the ball.** It was the simplest plan the other team could have had, and it worked. Our best player couldn't get the ball, and the rest of us couldn't get it back to her.

**The spacing broke down.** Not for one reason, but a collection of them.

**Some players were receiving the ball in the wrong places.** Parts of the floor they aren't comfortable playing from — spots where they haven't spent time, with the other team's pressure arriving at the same moment as the ball.

**And once they had it, they didn't yet have the skills to make the right play.** This is the part that's easy to miss. Standing in the right spot is one skill. Being comfortable there, and knowing what to do with the ball when it arrives, is another. A spot on the floor only helps a team if the player standing in it can use it.

## The Moment

There wasn't a single play. The moment was the pattern, repeating: the ball goes towards our best player, doesn't reach her, and nobody else is in a position to turn that into anything.

It looked like a team with one answer.

## What It Teaches

**This is "[Great Players Don't Create Space Alone](/blog/great-players-dont-create-space-alone)" from the other side.** I wrote that a star only shines because her team's spacing gives her somewhere to be. Game 4 was the reverse: take the star away, and see what the spacing is worth without her. Right now, not much — and that isn't a failure, it's information. A team that depends on one player getting the ball has a gravity problem in the wrong direction. The defence only has to solve one player.

**The answer isn't to force the ball to her.** When a defence overplays your best player, the fix is to become a threat everywhere else, so denying her costs them something. That takes players who are comfortable catching in different parts of the floor and deciding quickly — the same ground as [Playing Off the Gravity](/blog/playing-off-the-gravity), where the rest of the team has to be ready to use what one player creates. Here, the roles have to swap: the other girls need to be able to create it.

**Both halves of the problem are skills we've started.** Getting comfortable with the ball when pressure arrives is what [Session 2](/blog/session-2-protect-the-ball) was about. This game showed where it doesn't yet carry over: into uncomfortable spots, under real pressure.

## What We Did Next

We trained the day after, on the 2nd. The focus came straight out of the game: getting more comfortable with quick decisions, passing on the move, and maintaining momentum.

Quick decisions give a defence less time to deny anything. Passing on the move keeps the ball from stopping. And momentum is the opposite of what we saw on Sunday, when the ball kept arriving to someone standing still with nowhere to go.

## Game 5

We're on our way to it now. What I'll be watching for isn't the score — it's whether the quick decisions show up, and whether, when our best player is denied, someone else takes the ball and does something with it.
