## The Setup

Two of our girls had other commitments, and two new players filled in for them. They helped us out, and it changed the plan.

A lot of what we'd worked on at the last training needed players who'd been there for it. So that got put off, and we went back to the simple things: get back on defence, keep your hands straight up, and keep the pressure on until the other team makes a bad decision.

We won 28-12. As in Game 2, the score isn't the story.

## What I Saw

**They're starting to understand why we get back.** Not just the instruction, but the reason for it.

**But they still come out to meet the ball.** Once the other team gets over halfway, the instinct is to step up and go to the ball. Every step towards the ball is a step away from the space behind you. That's where the opposition attacked, and mostly off the dribble.

**Some of them are reading the key areas.** A few girls are starting to recognise where the important spots are and are beating the attacker there. That's real progress.

**Getting to the spot isn't enough on its own yet.** Until they can control the attacker's dribble, being in the right place still leaves too much space behind them. The spot buys you nothing if the dribbler goes straight past you into it.

## The Moment

In the second half, one player picked up two steals off exactly what we'd been working on.

Hands up, not reaching. Off the ball, reading the next pass. She read it, and she took it — twice.

That's the whole idea in two plays. It isn't about speed or reach. It's about reading the game one pass ahead, and she did it in a real game, not a drill.

## What It Teaches

**Defence is about the space behind you, not the ball in front of you.** When a defender comes out to meet the ball, she isn't wrong to want to pressure it — she just forgets what she's giving up. The open floor behind a defender who has stepped up is the same open floor a fast break lives on: space that exists because nobody's home. That's [transition space](/blog/the-five-kinds-of-space), and in this game it was the opposition using it against us.

**Pressure has to be disciplined pressure.** Keeping your hands straight up is what lets you stay in front without fouling — the reach-in that gives away [more than two points](/blog/the-foul-that-costs-more-than-two-points). And the player off the ball has the most important job: read the next pass. In Game 2 the idea [clicked in the fourth quarter](/blog/game-2-hands-up-eyes-open). This time one player made it happen twice.

**This is where the press comes from.** That instinct — knowing where the ball is going next and getting there first — is the foundation of a three-quarter-court and a half-court press. And every steal like those two is a turnover with the floor open in front of us, which is exactly when a [fast break](/blog/passing-ahead-in-transition) works.

## Next Session Focus

**Defence: use the sideline.** Force the dribbler towards the sideline wherever we can, and use the sideline as an extra defender. A player pushed to the sideline has half the floor taken away from her — that's how we start controlling the dribble instead of chasing it, and how getting to the right spot stops leaving the space behind us open.

**Offence: pivot, dribble, pass.** We'll keep building on the pivot. Pivot into a dribble that creates space, then pass — so the ball-handler makes her own room before she has to give it up.

**Then the rebound and the outlet.** Take the rebound above your head, and pass to the outlet box. I'll teach where the outlet box is and why we have it: the rebound is where our [transition](/blog/passing-ahead-in-transition) starts, and the outlet is the first pass of the break.
