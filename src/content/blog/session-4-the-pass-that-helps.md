## The Setup

We started with the same drills as [last session](/blog/session-3-keep-moving). The point wasn't to add new ones; it was to run them again and expand the same concepts. They're becoming a weekly routine, and a drill the girls already know is a drill they can think inside.

After that, we moved on to three new pieces.

## What We Worked On

**A shooting drill, kept simple.** Once again, nothing fancy. The point was two things: getting to the spot first, and the pass.

**Get to the spot first.** The shooter should already be there when the ball arrives, not arriving at the same moment. Everything about a shot gets better when the player isn't rushing to catch up with her own pass.

**Assist with the pass.** The idea is that a pass isn't just sending the ball in someone's general direction. When we pass it, we're trying to help the receiver *score*. That means thinking about where and how the ball arrives: can she catch it and shoot, or does she have to adjust and lose time? A good assist begins before the basket, at the pass.

**Three-on-three: a pass to the wing, then a screen away.** To finish, we played 3-on-3 with one pattern. The ball goes to the wing, and then the passer screens away. Instead of watching the pass, she goes to work for a teammate.

## What It Teaches

**The pass is part of the shot.** I wrote in [The Pass Before the Pass](/blog/the-pass-before-the-pass) that the assist gets the stat, but the pass before it won the possession. This is the other half of that: the pass that *is* the assist is a skill too. It's worth teaching a young player that how you deliver the ball can make or break her teammate's shot.

**Timing starts with the spot.** Getting to the spot before the ball is the same idea as [the run starting before the pass](/blog/the-run-starts-before-the-pass). The receiver's job is done before the pass is thrown, and the passer is throwing to where she'll be.

**Screening away is what you do instead of standing and watching.** After a pass, the most natural thing for a young player is to stop and watch it. Screening away gives her something to do immediately — which means she's still part of the play, and she's creating space for someone else. That's the habit that [great teams are built on](/blog/great-players-dont-create-space-alone).
